## Chapter walkthrough

### Hypothetical Scenario: Petrodollar Collapse
- A hypothetical scenario on March 15, 2026, depicts the potential collapse of the petrodollar system.
- A Pakistani tanker, the Karachi, is imagined transporting oil through the Strait of Hormuz, priced in Chinese yuan.
- This scenario directly challenges the 50-year-old agreement where six Gulf States sold oil exclusively in U.S. dollars in exchange for American military protection.
- A U.S./Israeli strike on Iran's supreme leader triggers regional attacks, including missile strikes on the six Gulf nations.
- This conflict is projected to shut down the world's largest oil and gas refineries, costing the region an estimated $1 billion daily.
- The Strait of Hormuz, a critical channel for one-fifth of the world's oil, is also shut down, stranding 150 ships.
- Such instability threatens the social contracts in these non-democratic states, which rely heavily on oil wealth and stability.

### Petrodollar Origins and Gulf Leverage
- The Gulf States collectively control nearly a third of global oil output and over half of the world's proven oil reserves.
- This significant leverage is reminiscent of the 1973 oil crisis, when Saudi-led nations imposed an embargo on the U.S. for supporting Israel.
- During the 1973 crisis, oil prices surged from $3 to $10 per barrel, contributing to U.S. inflation peaking at over 13%.
- The petrodollar agreement was formally established by the Nixon administration with Saudi Arabia by 1975.
- This agreement solidified the U.S. dollar's role in global oil trade, linking it to American security guarantees.

### Petrodollar Mechanics and US Advantages
- The petrodollar system forces any country buying oil from the Gulf to first purchase U.S. dollars, creating constant global demand for the currency.
- The U.S. dollar comprises around 60% of global foreign exchange reserves, primarily backed by this petrodollar agreement.
- The global oil industry is valued at $3.7 trillion annually, representing 3% of world GDP, with 90% of its trade occurring outside U.S. markets.
- This system has allowed the U.S. to run a trade deficit every year since 1976, accumulating $26 trillion in net foreign debt.
- It provides an effective subsidy of nearly $3,000 per American per year by maintaining dollar demand.
- Dollars earned by Gulf States are recycled back into the U.S. economy through the purchase of financial assets like Treasury bonds.
- By 1977, Saudi Arabia held 20% of all foreign-owned U.S. debt, and its official holdings were $117 billion in 2016, which helps keep U.S. borrowing costs low for its nearly $39 trillion national debt.

### Challenges to US Security Guarantees
- The U.S. is perceived as failing to uphold its security guarantees to Gulf States, weakening the core of the petrodollar agreement.
- A notable instance was the U.S.'s lack of response to a 2019 Iranian drone attack on Saudi oil facilities.
- In the hypothetical scenario, the U.S. also refuses to restock Qatar's Patriot missiles, further illustrating a perceived decline in commitment.
- Consequently, Gulf States are increasingly pivoting their strategic alliances and economic ties towards China.
- China is now Saudi Arabia's largest trading partner, indicating a significant shift in regional influence.

### Diversification and Petrodollar's Future
- The shift towards China includes currency swap agreements that bypass the U.S. dollar for trade.
- Gulf sovereign wealth funds, managing over $3 trillion, are actively diversifying their investments away from U.S. assets.
- This diversification signals a slow but steady erosion of the petrodollar system's dominance.
- While the U.S. is now the world's largest oil producer and less reliant on Gulf oil imports (10% today vs. one-third historically), its economic strength remains deeply tied to the dollar's reserve currency status.
- The long-term implications of this erosion could significantly impact global financial markets and geopolitical power dynamics.
