## Overview
- In this lecture, The speaker argues that india's 1991 economic reforms were a watershed moment, necessitated by a severe balance of payments crisis and decades of protectionist policies, leading to significant economic growth and integration into the global economy.

### Format and speakers
- Format: lecture.
- Speakers: Bank of England.

### Core argument
- India's 1991 economic reforms were a watershed moment, necessitated by a severe balance of payments crisis and decades of protectionist policies, leading to significant economic growth and integration into the global economy. However, these reforms also generated profound social costs, including increased inequality and rural distress, creating a complex and contested legacy.

## Chapter walkthrough

### The 1991 Balance of Payments Crisis
- In the summer of 1991, India faced a severe balance of payments crisis with foreign exchange reserves sufficient for only three weeks of essential imports.
- To secure an emergency IMF loan and prevent default, the government secretly airlifted 67 tons of gold to foreign banks as collateral.
- Public outrage over the gold transfer contributed to the collapse of the Chandra Shekhar government.
- This crisis was the culmination of 40 years of state-led, self-sufficiency policies implemented after India's independence in 1947.
- These policies, known as the "License Raj," required extensive government approval for nearly all business activities.

### Roots of the Crisis: The License Raj
- The "License Raj" system, established by acts like the IDRA 1951 and IPR 1956, mandated licenses for almost all business operations.
- Entrepreneurs often had to satisfy up to 80 different government agencies to conduct business.
- N.R. Narayana Murthy's experience of spending three years and over 50 trips to Delhi to import a single computer illustrates the bureaucratic hurdles.
- This system stifled competition, favored large corporations, and resulted in a sluggish "Hindu rate of growth" of 3-4% annually.
- Between 1950 and 1980, India's per capita economic growth was only 1% annually, significantly lagging behind nations like South Korea.

### Escalation and Political Instability
- By the mid-1980s, India's fiscal deficit worsened, rising from 8% of GDP to over 10.4% by 1990-91.
- External debt nearly doubled from $38 billion in 1985 to $84 billion by 1991, indicating growing financial vulnerability.
- The 1990 Gulf War triggered the crisis by increasing the oil import bill by $2 billion and cutting off crucial remittances.
- The collapse of the Soviet Union, India's largest trading partner, further exacerbated the economic situation.
- Political instability, including government collapses, a caretaker government, a Moody's credit downgrade, and Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, deepened the crisis.

### The 1991 Reforms and Key Figures
- P.V. Narasimha Rao became Prime Minister of a minority government and appointed economist Dr. Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister.
- On July 24, 1991, Dr. Singh delivered a historic budget speech outlining reforms based on Liberalisation, Privatization, and Globalisation (LPG).
- The reforms dismantled the "License Raj" by abolishing licensing for all but 18 strategic sectors.
- Public sector monopolies were significantly reduced from 17 to 8 industries, opening up more sectors to private enterprise.
- Key measures included devaluing the rupee by nearly 20%, slashing import tariffs, and raising the foreign equity limit from 40% to 51%.

### Immediate Economic Rebound
- The Indian economy rebounded significantly, with growth increasing from 1.06% in 1991 to 5.48% in 1992.
- The average economic growth for the decade following the reforms exceeded 6.5% annually.
- Foreign investment surged dramatically from $73.5 million in 1991 to $2.14 billion by 1996.
- Foreign exchange reserves were successfully rebuilt from a critical low of $5.6 billion, stabilizing the economy.
- These reforms also created the foundational environment for the rapid growth and success of India's information technology sector.

### Long-term Consequences and Criticisms
- The reforms contributed to a "Billionaire Raj" and extreme economic inequality in India.
- A study co-authored by Thomas Piketty indicated that by 2022, the top 1% held over 40% of wealth, the highest share since 1922.
- Oxfam reported that the top 10% of the population holds 77% of national wealth, surpassing levels seen during the British colonial era.
- Reduced agricultural investment exposed farmers to global market volatility, leading to a severe debt crisis.
- P. Sainath documented nearly 400,000 farmer suicides between 1995 and 2018, highlighting the rural distress.
- The economy experienced "jobless growth," with employment elasticity falling significantly post-reform.
- Stagnant social spending led to increased out-of-pocket health expenses and reliance on private education, exacerbating social divides.

## Closing remarks
- Recap: The 1991 reforms successfully averted an economic collapse and propelled India into a new era of growth and global integration. However, this progress came at a substantial social cost, marked by widening inequality and distress among vulnerable populations, leaving a complex and dual legacy that continues to shape modern India.
